## Abstract

As Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly function as global social actors, their hidden internal value atlas, i.e., the internal norms, ethics, and cultural priorities that guide their behaviors, remains largely unexamined, risking the projection of a singular worldview onto a diverse global population. Here, we present a comprehensive audit of human values across 20 LLMs and 66 countries and regions (representing 62% of the global population), using the World Value Survey (WVS) Wave 7 as an empirical benchmark. Our analysis reveals four systemic distortions in this value atlas. First, LLM-embedded values are significantly more inclined toward Secular-rational and Self-expression values than the actual populations they represent, often erasing the traditional and survival-oriented values prevalent in many regions. Second, we find a stark divergence in value representation based on language; a country's values shift significantly when the model is prompted in English versus the its native language. Third, we demonstrate a pervasive pattern of digital Orientalism, where non-Western cultures are represented with higher fidelity via English-language prompting, whereas Western cultural values are best captured in their native languages. Finally, we show that the alignment of a region's values in LLMs may be associated with its colonial history, indicating that historical power dynamics may be structurally codified into modern AI architectures. These findings suggest that current LLMs do not merely reflect the world; they re-map it through a specific cultural and historical lens. We discuss the implications of this value atlas for the global deployment of AI and the urgent need for cultural plurality in future model development.

## 1 Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used to address grand societal challenges through human-AI partnership or as social actors (). They now function as active intermediaries in the human social interactions (), influencing how information is produced, accessed, and interpreted across diverse cultural landscapes and human value systems. As LLMs advise on public policy, generate cultural narratives, and mediate global interpersonal communication via real-time translation and automated drafting (), they cease to be neutral instruments (). Instead, they operate according to a hidden "Value Atlas"—a complex, encoded system of value priorities and social norms. Since these models are largely trained on data reflecting specific linguistic and geopolitical centers, it inevitably results in biased learning about low-resource cultures primarily from outside observers writing in a few languages (1). Thus, their global deployment risks a form of digital homogenization, where a singular worldview is projected onto a pluralistic population. Despite their role as growingly important players of modern social exchange, the internal cartography of values in LLMs remains a critical gap, necessitating a rigorous empirical understanding of the value landscapes embedded in these models.

Addressing this critical gap in our understanding requires moving beyond qualitative anecdotes toward a robust, empirical framework capable of quantifying the value orientations of artificial intelligence. In this study, we bridge the divide between computational linguistics and human value research by conducting a systematic audit of the hidden values within 20 Large Language Models, ranging from proprietary state-of-the-art systems to widely accessible open-source architectures. To establish a rigorous ground truth, we utilize the World Value Survey (WVS) Wave 7 (), the gold standard in longitudinal human value research, as our comparative benchmark. Our methodology involves extracting model-embedded values across 66 countries and regions representing 62% of the world's population, using prompts in both English and native languages, thereby capturing a vast spectrum of socio-economic and geopolitical contexts. We then simulate "Value Atlas" that these LLMs project to their global users using WVS' two-dimensional value space—contrasting Traditional versus Secular-Rational values and Survival versus Self-Expression values. This approach allows us to move beyond simple bias detection, enabling a systematic investigation into LLMs' latent value system and identities in relevant to human values around the world. In doing so, we provide a comprehensive map of the value landscape of modern AI, revealing the hidden tensions between technological alignment and global pluralism.

Our findings demonstrate a systematic and multifaceted divergence between AI-encoded values and the actual human populations they represent, revealing that LLM's Value Atlas is deeply and structurally skewed. First, we show that LLMs exhibit a pronounced and consistent lean toward Secular-rational and Self-expression values, significantly over-representing these orientations relative to the empirical benchmarks of the 66 countries and regions surveyed. This bias indicates a pervasive ideological drift that risks marginalizing the traditional and survival-oriented perspectives that remain central to a vast majority of the global population. Second, we identify a profound linguistic fracture: a LLM's value profile shifts radically depending on whether it is prompted in English or a country's native language. Third, such a divergence facilitates a phenomenon we define as "digital Orientalism"—a systemic pattern where non-Western cultures are paradoxically represented with higher fidelity under English-language prompting, while Western cultures maintain superior representation in their native languages. Finally, we find that these value distributions may be historically rooted with a case study of the Confucian cultural zone which shows that a country or region's colonial history may be associated with its value alignment within these models.

Collectively, these results suggest that LLMs do not simply mirror the world's value; instead, they structurally re-map it through a specific cultural and historical lens, often reinforcing existing geopolitical power dynamics. By uncovering these structural asymmetries, this study provides a necessary empirical foundation for the development of pluralistic value-based AI alignment, challenging the industry to move beyond monolithic value frameworks toward a more representative and adaptive future.

### Study 1: Pervasive Secular-Rational Bias

We began by exploring the general value systems of these LLMs, intentionally avoiding the assignment of specific national personas to capture the models' latent cultural "defaults." To map these orientations, we adapted ten indicators from the World Values Survey (WVS) to evaluate two primary dimensions of human culture: Traditional versus Secular-rational values, and Survival versus Self-expression values. Each indicator's corresponding question was encapsulated within a prompt in English and translated to the other five official languages of the United Nations—Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish by CATTI-licensed professional translators (see SI). Here, we had 120 unique model-language combinations (20 models × 6 languages).

As illustrated in Fig. 1, we mapped these model orientations against the value landscapes of 66 target countries and regions from WVS Wave 7 (the semi-transparent background in Fig. 1). Our results reveal a striking convergence: nearly all models exhibit a dominant orientation toward the Secular-rational and Self-expression quadrant (the top-right of the Inglehart–Welzel cultural map). However, this alignment is highly sensitive to linguistic framing. We observed substantial shifts along the Traditional versus Secular-rational axis depending on the prompt language. On average, English prompts yielded the most pronounced Secular-rational responses (mean = 2.66), with French (mean = 2.87) and Spanish (mean = 2.78) following closely. In contrast, while still leaning toward Secular-rational values, Arabic prompts elicited significantly more traditional values (mean = 1.06), representing a 1.60-unit divergence from English-language outputs (one-way ANOVA: F = 3.35, p = 0.007). Notably, such a linguistic influence was dimension-specific; we found no significant differences among languages regarding the Survival versus Self-expression dimension (F = 0.86, p = 0.51), suggesting that while language can shift a model's "moral" or "traditional" outlook, its orientation toward survival vs. self-expression remains relatively fixed.

### Study 2: Language Divergence

Building on our initial observation that language acts as a latent variable in value consistency with empirical WVS benchmarks (Study 1), we sought to systematically quantify the magnitude of this divergence across a global scope. In Study 2, we moved beyond generic prompting to a persona-based approach: we instructed 20 distinct LLMs to adopt the persona of a "typical citizen" from each of the 66 target countries and regions. We then queried these models using both English and the country or region's official/native language(s), generating approximately 2,640 unique model–country–language combinations (see Materials and Methods). To examine the value representation in LLMs, we calculated cultural consistency as the Euclidean distance between the model-derived value coordinates and the empirical WVS benchmarks. Thus, smaller distances indicate a higher fidelity to the actual human population.

The aggregate results reveal a statistically significant, albeit modest, "English Advantage." Across the 46 non–English-speaking countries and regions in our sample, prompting in English produced value representations that were, on average, 4.6% closer to the survey benchmarks than prompting in the native tongue (paired t-test: t = 2.85, p = 0.007, Cohen's d = 0.42). However, this aggregate mean conceals a striking heterogeneity. As shown in Fig. 2, the distribution of the English advantage is heavily right-skewed with a long tail. While the mean advantage was +5.8% with a median at +7.7%, indicating that for a subset of nations, the reliance on English is disproportionately high. For example, Germany exhibited the most robust native-language advantage (mean = -26.1%), implying that native language captures German values far better than English. In contrast, Tunisia displayed the most extreme English advantage (mean = +27.3%), suggesting a systemic failure of the model to capture Tunisian values in Arabic. This profound divergence—where some nations are "native-readable" while others are only "English-readable"—led us to investigate the structural causes of this disparity.

### Study 3: Digital Orientalism

The divergence observed in Study 2 motivated us to examine whether such a divergence was associated with particular cultural and geopolitical patterns. We thus grouped the 46 non-English-speaking countries and regions in our sample by cultural regions in Inglehart–Welzel classifications, which are: African-Islamic, Catholic Europe, Confucian, Latin America, Orthodox/Slavic, Protestant Europe. We then examined English advantages across these regions. The results revealed two interesting patterns (Tab. 1). Non-Western regions showed substantial English advantages, with African-Islamic countries showing the largest effect (mean = 15.3%, p < 0.001). In sharp contrast, Western European regions showed native-language advantages, with Protestant Europe showing the strongest effect (mean = -21.8%, p = 0.004). Geographically, English's advantage gradually reduces from the Arabic countries in the Middle East to the East Asian countries and regions (see Fig 3). Such a pattern was remarkably consistent across models. For African-Islamic countries, 16 of 20 models showed positive English advantage; for Orthodox Europe, 12 of 20 models did. The Western versus non-Western contrast yielded a large effect size (Cohen's d = 2.24) and remained robust under alternative specifications and distance metrics (SI Appendix).

In his classic "Orientalism," Edward W. Said argued that: "The Orient, in short, existed as a set of values attached, not to its modern realities, but to a series of valorized contacts it had had with a distant European past. This is a pure example of textual, schematic attitude I have been referring to" (pp. 85). He further pointed out that the West "knows" the East better than the East knows itself, because the West controls the discourse about the East. Our observation that English prompts yield higher-fidelity representations of non-Western values than native-language prompts offers a profound empirical parallel to Edward Said's theory of Orientalism. Said argued that the "Orient" was socially constructed through Western discourse, a system where the truth about non-Western cultures was established not by the populations themselves, but by Western scholars, authors, and administrators.

In the context of LLMs, our results thus indicate a form of Digital Orientalism. LLMs are the ultimate textual attitude machines who do not experience the world but learn from text. Because these models are trained on vast English-centric corpora that describe, analyze, and categorize the world, they appear to encode the truth of non-Western values primarily through a Western lens. When prompted in English, the model accesses this vast, structured Western archive of knowledge about the Orient, resulting in a high alignment with empirical benchmarks (WVS). Conversely, when prompted in the native language, the model's representation degrades, suggesting that the native language is treated as a less authoritative source of cultural knowledge—even regarding its own culture.

This phenomenon operationalizes Said's critique: the non-Western world is rendered as an object of study accessible through English, rather than a subject capable of defining itself through its own linguistic agency. Consequently, English functions not merely as a neutral medium of translation, but as an epistemic filter that determines what could be accepted as valid, relevant, or true (2, 3). Thus, LLMs impose a structural dependency where non-Western cultural identity is most legible to them only when mediated by the hegemonic language, i.e., English.

### Parallel French Advantage

To further validate Digital Orientalism beyond the English context, we examined whether a parallel French Advantage exists, testing whether value distortions stem from a broad Western hegemonic lens rather than English data volume alone. Focusing on **12 Arabic-speaking nations in the African-Islamic region** (Algeria, Egypt, Iraq, Jordan, Kuwait, Lebanon, Libya, Morocco, Palestine, Qatar, Tunisia, Yemen), we compared value orientations extracted via native Arabic versus French prompts across 20 models. The results reveal a marginal French Advantage where French prompts yielded representations slightly closer to empirical benchmarks than native Arabic prompts (mean = -5.4%, paired t-test: p = 0.12, not statistically significant). This null-to-weak finding suggests that while there may be partial alignment with Western knowledge frameworks at the expense of indigenous Arab epistemologies, the effect is substantially weaker than the English Advantage, supporting the interpretation that English's global linguistic dominance—not merely "Western language" effects—drives the primary Digital Orientalism pattern.

### The Geography of the Textual Gaze: From Near to Far Orient

Our results further reveal a spatial gradient to Digital Orientalism. The English Advantage, i.e., the phenomenon where English prompts paradoxically yield higher-fidelity value representations, is most pronounced in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) which is significantly overlap with the African-Islamic cultural region) and decays significantly as we move toward East Asia which belongs to Confucian cultural region. Such a distance decay mirrors the geopolitical stratification described by Said, who distinguished between the Near Orient (the immediate, familiar "Other" of Europe) and the Far Orient (distant and distinct). The MENA region has historically been the primary fixation of the Orientalist project—subjected to centuries of intense Western textualization, categorization, and study due to its proximity and biblical significance. Consequently, the English-language training corpora are saturated with authoritative descriptions of Arab and Islamic values, creating a high-fidelity Orientalist mirror that the LLM accesses when prompted in English.

In contrast, the Far Orient e.g., China, Japan, while subject to imperialism, maintained a greater degree of discursive autonomy within the Western canon due to the absence of substantial colonial holdings by western countries. As Said pointed out, Western scholars did not found it important to "bring Indochina into the domain of Orientalism" (pp. 218) until late 1800s. The English Advantage shrinks in these regions because the Western textual archive does not dominate the definition of their values as totally as it does for the Near Orient. This suggests that LLMs' value alignment is a function of discursive density. The more intensely a culture has been Othered by Western scholarship, the more the model relies on English to define its truth.

### Model Origin Effects

To determine whether the observed value distortions were artifacts of specific model development environments, we analyzed the influence of model origin on the magnitude of digital Orientalism (Fig. 3C). Our analysis of models developed in the United States (n = 13), China (n = 5), and Europe (n = 2) revealed a striking qualitative consistency: no matter where they were developed, all models exhibited the same structural bias—non-Western regions showed significant English Advantages, while Western Europe maintained superior representation in native tongues.

However, quantitative variations emerged where training data density was likely highest. China-developed models demonstrated a near-zero English advantage for East Asian countries and regions (mean = -0.2%) compared to their US-developed counterparts (mean = +6.9%), a finding consistent with their greater exposure to high-quality, native Chinese linguistic data. Yet, such a home-field advantage did not extend to other non-Western regions. For the African-Islamic region, all three model groups displayed high English advantages (US: +13.8%, China: +22.3%, Europe: +7.3%). The Western versus non-Western contrast remained large and statistically significant within US-developed (p < 0.001) and China-developed (p < 0.001) model groups. This universality suggests that digital Orientalism is a robust, cross-model phenomenon—a systemic property of how modern LLMs ingest global information, rather than a bias attributable to any single development team or national agenda.

### Beyond Cultural and Linguistic Distance: The Specificity of the Orientalist Gaze

To determine if the observed "English Advantage" was merely a proxy for general cultural dissimilarity or linguistic complexity, we tested two alternative hypotheses: (1) simple cultural distance from the West and (2) language family membership. First, we examined whether the fidelity of English prompting simply decays as a country becomes culturally distinct from the West. Using Euclidean distance on the Inglehart-Welzel Cultural Map as a proxy for "cultural distance" from Protestant Europe, we found a very weak correlation with the English advantage (Pearson r = 0.09, p = 0.003; Spearman ρ = 0.20, p < 0.001), explaining less than 1% of the variance. This near-null result is theoretically significant: it indicates that the English Advantage is not a linear function of how different a culture is. A country or region does not become better represented in English simply because it is culturally distant; rather, this effect appears driven by specific historical dynamics of representation. Second, we investigated whether the effect was an artifact of linguistic typology. Our analysis of language families yielded inconsistent predictors. We observed native-language penalties for Arabic-speaking countries (-6.1%) and Slavic-speaking countries (-72.0%, p = 0.023), but found no statistically significant effects for the Romance (-16.6%), Germanic (-81.4%), or CJK (Chinese-Japanese-Korean) families (-21.0%).

These findings strongly reinforce the Digital Orientalism hypothesis over purely technical explanations. If the English Advantage were merely about translation difficulty, we would expect consistent patterns across linguistically distant families (e.g., CJK). Instead, the specific, intense English Advantage observed in the Arabic-speaking world (distinct from CJK) supports Said's contention that the West's textual domination is not uniform but is geographically specific. The Near Orient (African-Islamic) is subject to a unique form of discursive capture that does not apply equally to the Far Orient or to culturally distant but non-colonized regions. Thus, the bias in LLMs is not a generic error of foreignness, but a specific artifact of historical discursive density.

### Study 4: Colonial Legacies

**Intra-Regional Stratification of the English Advantage in East Asia**

While the English Advantage is generally attenuated across the Confucian cultural region compared to the African-Islamic region, the internal variance within this cluster offers a compelling natural experiment. Here, distinct entities share a foundational linguistic and ethical heritage yet diverge sharply in their colonial and geopolitical trajectories (Fig. ). Our results illuminate how these historical divergences are encoded into LLMs' value bias.

Among the six entities analyzed, Taiwan exhibited the strongest English advantage (mean = 27.1), followed closely by Hong Kong (mean = 20.6). In Hong Kong, this alignment appears to be a direct legacy of 150 years of British colonial rule, which institutionalized English as the primary medium for law, commerce, and higher education. In Taiwan, the effect reflects a different vector: despite 50 years of Japanese occupation, its value alignment correlates strongly with deep post-war geopolitical integration with the United States. South Korea (mean = 3.0) and Japan (mean = 3.3) show modest English advantages.

Conversely, China (mean = 0.07), i.e., the nation that retained relatively great political sovereignty and modernized on autonomous terms, displays minimal English advantage. In a revealing contrast, Macao, a Portuguese colony for four centuries, recorded a negative English Advantage (mean = -5.3), mirroring the value profiles found in Catholic Europe. This intra-regional stratification suggests that the 'Value Atlas' of LLMs is not a reflection of generic cultural distance, but a precise historical record of geopolitical intimacy with specific Western powers.

**English and French Colonies in Sub-Saharan Africa**

Sub-Saharan Africa presents a critical test case for disentangling direct colonial legacies from the broader epistemic hegemony of the English language. Our analysis of British and French post-colonial countries reveals a two-tiered system of value distortion, where English acts not merely as a neutral medium, but as a normative filter that re-aligns African values toward Western defined values.

We first examined six British-colonized nations (Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, Zimbabwe, Zambia) where English remains the dominant official medium. Even within these shared linguistic countries, we observed a systematic directional bias toward Western modernity. On the Traditional–Secular Rational dimension (PC2), the models exhibited a consistent secularizing tendency. Four of the six nations showed a pronounced upward shift away from traditional values: South Africa (mean = +1.71), Zimbabwe (mean = +0.83), Ghana (mean = +0.75), and Nigeria (mean = +0.56). Only Zambia (mean = -0.62) and Kenya (mean = -0.29) resisted this trend. While the aggregate mean bias (mean = +0.49) did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.21), the directionality is telling: for the majority of these nations, the English prompt effectively modernizes the value representation, pulling it closer to the secular norms of the Global North.

The power of English becomes even more evident when examining the French colonial sphere. We analyzed Mali and Burkina Faso—nations where French is the official language and the direct colonial legacy. Counter-intuitively, these nations did not show a native-language (French) advantage. Instead, they exhibited a strong English Advantage: Burkina Faso (mean = +17.5%) and Mali (mean = +17.6%), with a mean advantage of +17.5%. This result challenges the simple "colonial mirror" hypothesis. If models simply reflected colonial training data, French should yield higher fidelity. However, the superiority of English suggests that: in digitally low-resource environments (like the Sahel), the model relies less on sparse local/colonial data (French) and more on the massive volume of international discourse (security reports, NGO studies, global news) produced in English.

Synthesizing these findings, we observe that Digital Orientalism in Africa functions through a hierarchy of information. In countries which are former British colonies, English serves as a normative filter, subtly secularizing traditional values. In countries which are former French colonies, particularly for marginalized nations, English overrides even the specific colonial language to become the primary lens through which the LLMs understand these populations. Thus, for Sub-Saharan Africa, the value atlas is constructed not on the ground, but from the vantage point of the Anglophone world.

**Economic Scale and Linguistic Hegemony in Latin America**

To further disentangle the influence of colonial legacy, we compared English advantages across Latin American nations, grouped by their primary colonial languages: Spanish (n = 11), Portuguese (n = 1, Brazil), and French (n = 1, Haiti). The results highlight a complex interplay between linguistic heritage and economic scale. Haiti, the sole French-colonized nation in the dataset, exhibited the most pronounced English advantage (+20.4%), suggesting a significant disconnection between the native context and the model's internal representation.

However, the Spanish-colonized block averaged a modest positive effect (mean = +5.4%) and displayed substantial internal heterogeneity driven by economic and geopolitical stature. Smaller countries such as Bolivia (mean = +19.8%), Nicaragua (mean = +17.4%), and Ecuador (mean = +13.2%) showed English advantages, consistent with the pattern of digital peripheries relying on English for accurate representation. Meanwhile, larger or more digitally integrated economies, including Venezuela (mean = -9.9%), Uruguay (mean = -5.1%), Peru (mean = -2.2%), and Argentina (mean = -2.7%) demonstrated a Spanish advantage similar to countries and regions in Catholic Europe, indicating a sufficiently robust native-language corpus to override the English default. Brazil (Portuguese) occupied a middle ground with a minimal positive effect (mean = +3.6%).

## Discussion

Large Language Models (LLMs) do not merely reflect the world's cultural diversity; they structurally re-map it through a specific historical and linguistic lens. Previous research has well-established that LLMs exhibit a "WEIRD" (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) bias, often aligning with American or Western European norms even when prompted in neutral contexts (). However, our findings advance this discourse by identifying the mechanism of this distortion: it is not merely a passive over-representation of Western data, but an active, language-dependent re-mapping of non-Western identities. We define this phenomenon as Digital Orientalism, where non-Western cultures are paradoxically represented with higher fidelity when prompted in English than in their native languages.

This finding provides a profound empirical parallel to Edward Said's critique of colonial knowledge production, where the West "knows" the East better than the East knows itself because the West controls the discourse. While scholars like Mohamed et al. (4) and Couldry & Mejias (5) have theorized data colonialism as a continuation of imperial extraction, our study offers quantitative evidence that this coloniality is encoded in the LLMs' epistemic architecture. Unlike recent studies on cultural erasure which focus on performance degradation in low-resource languages (6), we demonstrate a normative shift: English functions as an epistemic filter. The models access a structured "Western archive" of knowledge about the non-Western world, treating English descriptions as authoritative while treating native-language data, which is often sparse or non-aligned, as secondary. This enriches observations of digital Orientalism in online discourse around Asian "tech nations" (7), extending the critique into the domain of complex value systems around the world.

This "textual attitude" creates a distinct geography of bias that mirrors historical colonialism. We find a spatial gradient to Digital Orientalism: the English Advantage is most intense in the African-Islamic region, where centuries of Western academic focus have saturated the training corpora with English descriptions of Arab values. As we move to the Far Orient, this advantage decays, consistent with theories of varying amount of data in native texts. Furthermore, our results regarding the inter-regional stratification of English Advantage in Confucian cultural region, challenge the assumption that scaling data alone resolves bias. Instead, it supports Sambasivan et al.'s argument that fairness in AI is often contextually hollow unless it accounts for the specific power dynamics of the Global South (8).

Moreover, this bias is systemic, transcending the origin of any single model. Whether developed in the US, China, or Europe, all models exhibit the same structural prejudice against the African-Islamic world. This universality aligns with Hershcovich et al.'s warning that cross-cultural NLP often defaults to a superficial translation of Western concepts rather than true cultural adaptation (9). Even more telling is the "Hegemonic Default" observed in the Sahel (e.g., Mali, Burkina Faso), where models revert to English as the primary source of truth due to data scarcity. This validates the concerns regarding the dangers of "stochastic parrots" amplifying hegemonic viewpoints when training data is unbalanced (10).

The structural asymmetries identified here pose a risk of value homogenization. As LLMs increasingly function as social actors (11) in human-AI interaction, they threaten to create a feedback loop where users from the Global South are nudged toward Anglophone norms to be understood by the algorithms and models. Hence, Digital Orientalism could harden into a normative standard. Addressing this requires moving beyond de-biasing or simple cultural alignment toward value pluralistic AI, where models are designed to sustain the rich multiplicity of human experience.

## Limitations

The reliance on the World Values Survey (WVS) Wave 7 introduces inherent constraints. While a gold standard, it remains subject to self-reporting biases and translation nuances. Furthermore, the Inglehart-Welzel map theoretically compresses complex cultures into two dimensions. Thus, the observed alignment may reflect the model's ability to reproduce survey responses rather than lived behavioral reality. Future work should triangulate these findings with behavioral benchmarks to verify if the "Value Atlas" translates to real-world decision-making.

A central epistemological challenge lies in semantically distinguishing authentic representation from accurate stereotyping. High alignment with WVS benchmarks might indicate a successful simulation of a citizen's cognitive state or merely the retrieval of a high-fidelity statistical caricature, a mechanism central to our Digital Orientalism hypothesis. Without access to proprietary training sets, we cannot fully disentangle the model's knowledge of a culture from its schematic alignment with it.

Finally, we assume temporal correspondence between WVS Wave 7 (2017–2022) and model training cutoffs (2021–2024), treating dynamic cultural values as static snapshots. Rapid societal shifts may not be reflected in either the survey baseline or the model's frozen weights. Additionally, while we analyzed 66 countries and regions, the English Advantage may function differently in regions with negligible digital footprints, where the Hegemonic Default could be even more pronounced.
