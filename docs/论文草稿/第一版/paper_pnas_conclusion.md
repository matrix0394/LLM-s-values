# Conclusion (PNAS Format - v3.0)

**Target length: ~260 words**
**Status**: v3.0 - Updated with baseline language effect and multi-mechanism framework

---

This study provides the first systematic quantification of language-dependent cultural bias in large language models, revealing two distinct layers of language effects. First, at the baseline level, the language of prompting itself systematically shifts models' expressed values: English prompts elicit the most secular responses (PC1=+2.69), while Arabic prompts elicit the most traditional responses (PC1=+0.98), a significant difference (F=3.38, p=0.007). Second, at the cultural knowledge level, we discover a striking pattern we term "digital Orientalism": across 23 models, 66 countries, and 13 languages, LLMs represent non-Western cultures more accurately in English than in native languages (Islamic/Arab +17.4%, Orthodox/Slavic +17.7%), while Western European cultures show the opposite pattern (-11.5% native advantage).

This asymmetry is not uniform but reflects multiple interacting mechanisms. Training data availability creates dramatic heterogeneity: China shows near-perfect language parity (+0.07%), while Taiwan shows strong English advantage (+27.1%). Colonial legacy matters: Hong Kong's British history correlates with English advantage (+24.8%), while Macao's Portuguese history left minimal imprint (-13.6% Simplified Chinese advantage). Cultural soft power provides partial mitigation: Japan (+3.3%) and Korea (+3.0%) show minimal English advantage due to cultural exports generating authentic English-language knowledge. Language variants create intra-linguistic Orientalism: European Spanish/Portuguese training data fails to transfer to Latin American variants. Model origin creates regional specialization: Chinese models show weaker biases for Sino-Tibetan languages (+1.9% vs +16.8% for US models) but not elimination.

These findings challenge the intuitive assumption that native language always yields better cultural accuracy. For non-Western cultures, English often provides more accurate representations—a counterintuitive result with profound implications for global AI deployment. The pattern is not inevitable: Chinese models demonstrate that diversifying training data reduces bias. However, addressing digital Orientalism requires systemic changes beyond simply translating interfaces or diversifying model origins.

We propose three concrete steps forward. First, AI developers should prioritize high-quality native-language content from underrepresented regions, particularly for non-Western cultures where English currently dominates. Second, evaluation frameworks should assess cultural competence across languages, not just translation quality or task performance. Third, transparency about training data composition should become standard practice, enabling identification of knowledge asymmetries.

As AI systems mediate cross-cultural communication for billions of users, ensuring they represent all cultures equitably is not merely a technical challenge but a moral imperative. Our methodology provides a foundation for measuring progress toward this goal, enabling researchers and developers to quantify cultural representation and track improvements across model generations.
