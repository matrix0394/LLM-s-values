# Materials and Methods

## Overview

We organized the analyses into four studies aligned with the four claims in the Abstract. Study 1 estimates LLMs’ intrinsic value orientations and summarizes their location relative to survey benchmarks. Study 2 quantifies language-dependent differences in country role-play representations, comparing English prompting versus official-language prompting. Study 3 tests whether language effects exhibit a regional reversal pattern consistent with “digital Orientalism.” Study 4 conducts pre-specified case comparisons (Hong Kong vs Macao; Latin America) to examine whether language effects vary across contexts linked to colonial-language exposure.

## Common Framework and Data (Studies 1–4)

**Cultural map and ground truth.** We locate both survey-measured and model-expressed values in the Inglehart–Welzel cultural map: PC1 (Survival vs Self-Expression) and PC2 (Traditional vs Secular-Rational). Ground-truth coordinates are taken from the Integrated Values Survey (IVS), which integrates World Values Survey Wave 7 (2017–2022) and European Values Study data. The IVS dataset covers 109 countries; our analysis focuses on 66 countries with available IVS coordinates (country list in SI). Under the WVS/IVS population-coverage definition used in our pipeline, this 66-country subset represents approximately 62% of the world population.

**Instrument.** All studies use the same ten WVS items commonly used to construct the Inglehart–Welzel map: happiness (A008), interpersonal trust (A165), respect for authority (E018), political action (E025), importance of God (F063), attitudes toward homosexuality (F118) and abortion (F120), national pride (G006), post-materialism (Y002), and autonomy (Y003). Models are required to output numeric-only responses in fixed schemas (single-choice integer or constrained multi-choice list) to enable consistent coding.

**Models.** We evaluate 23 large language models (SI Appendix, Table S1). Models are accessed via API endpoints between February and December 2025 using temperature = 0.1. For country role-play analyses (Studies 2–4), two small models (Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen-2.5-3B) are excluded due to systematically low compliance with numeric-only survey formats (quality rule specified below), leaving 21 models.

**Languages.** Role-play evaluations are conducted in English and in the target country’s official language(s). The full list of evaluated languages and the country–language mapping rules (including multilingual countries such as Switzerland and Belgium) are provided in SI.

**Response validation and repetitions.** Each questionnaire run is repeated five times per configuration. For each item we take the modal response across repetitions as the final answer; for list-type items (autonomy), selections are sorted before mode calculation to ensure order invariance. Outputs are validated against strict schemas; invalid outputs (extra text, out-of-range values, wrong cardinality) are rejected and re-issued up to three times. A configuration is retained if at least six of the ten items are valid after retries.

**Projection and alignment metric.** Validated responses are coded using IVS/WVS rules and projected into the Inglehart–Welzel space using probabilistic principal component analysis (PPCA) trained on IVS ground-truth data (two components; Varimax rotation; rescaling to align with IVS coordinates; details in SI). Cultural alignment is quantified as Euclidean distance between model-derived and IVS coordinates; smaller distances indicate better alignment.

## Study 1. Intrinsic Value Orientations of Large Language Models

**Research question.** What intrinsic value orientations do LLMs express in the absence of any assigned national identity, and how do these coordinates compare with survey benchmarks?

**Design.** We present the ten items without assigning any country, nationality, or cultural identity. Models receive a system prompt instructing them to act as a survey response system and to output only numeric answers under strict formatting requirements.

To examine baseline language dependence, we administer the questionnaire in six official United Nations languages: Arabic (ar), Simplified Chinese (zh-cn), English (en), French (fr), Russian (ru), and Spanish (es). Each of the 23 models is evaluated in all six languages (138 model–language combinations), with five repetitions per combination.

**Analysis.** Responses are coded and projected into the Inglehart–Welzel space using the common PPCA procedure. We summarize:

1. the distribution of model coordinates across languages, and
2. language effects using one-way ANOVA on PC1 and PC2 across the six languages, followed by Tukey HSD post-hoc comparisons.
    To describe within-model stability, we compute each model’s coordinate range and standard deviation across the six languages (PC1 and PC2 separately).

**Primary outcome measures.** Projected coordinates (PC1,PC2)(PC1, PC2)(PC1,PC2) for each model–language combination, and within-model cross-language variability (range/SD). Full prompts and coding rules are in SI.

## Study 2. Language-Dependent Differences in Cultural Representation

**Research question.** Do LLMs’ country role-play representations differ between English prompting and official-language prompting?

**Design.** We instruct models to role-play as typical adult citizens of target countries and answer the same ten items from that perspective. For non–English-speaking countries (n = 45), we run matched evaluations under (i) English prompting and (ii) official-language prompting to enable within-country comparisons. For English-speaking countries (n = 21), we evaluate in English only; these countries do not contribute to the paired English-vs-official contrast.

Multilingual countries are evaluated separately for each major official language (e.g., Switzerland: German/French/Italian). For paired language analyses, we aggregate within a country across its official-language interfaces (rules in SI) to avoid over-weighting multilingual countries.

Two small models (Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen-2.5-3B) are excluded from role-play analyses because they consistently fail to satisfy numeric-only schemas even after retries; all Study 2 analyses use the remaining 21 models.

**Analysis.** For each model mmm and each non–English-speaking country ccc, we compute cultural distances under official-language prompting and English prompting:

Dm,c,official,Dm,c,English.D_{m,c,\text{official}},\quad D_{m,c,\text{English}}.Dm,c,official,Dm,c,English.

We define the model-level paired distance difference:

ΔDm,c=Dm,c,official−Dm,c,English.\Delta D_{m,c}= D_{m,c,\text{official}} - D_{m,c,\text{English}}.ΔDm,c=Dm,c,official−Dm,c,English.

We also define a model-level English advantage percentage:

Am,c=100×Dm,c,official−Dm,c,EnglishDm,c,official.A_{m,c} = 100 \times \frac{D_{m,c,\text{official}} - D_{m,c,\text{English}}}{D_{m,c,\text{official}}}.Am,c=100×Dm,c,officialDm,c,official−Dm,c,English.

1. **Paired tests by country.** For each country ccc, we test whether ΔDm,c\Delta D_{m,c}ΔDm,c differs from zero across models (paired t-test; nonparametric paired alternative in SI).
2. **Consistency across models.** For each country, we compute the proportion of models with ΔDm,c>0\Delta D_{m,c}>0ΔDm,c>0.

4. **Regional aggregation (descriptive + tests).** We aggregate country-level English advantage within pre-specified cultural regions (region membership in SI) and test whether regional means differ from zero.

## Study 3. Digital Orientalism in Non-Western Cultural Representation

**Research question.** Do language effects exhibit a systematic regional reversal, where non-Western regions show positive English advantage while Western Europe shows official-language advantage?

**Design.** Using the English advantage estimates from Study 2 (restricted to the 45 non–English-speaking countries), we test whether language effects differ between Western and non-Western groupings and across cultural regions.

We define Western vs non-Western using pre-specified region labels (membership in SI) rather than post hoc country selection. Region labels follow an established cultural-region classification used throughout the manuscript (e.g., Western Europe, Latin America, Confucian, Islamic/Arab, etc.; full mapping in SI).

**Analysis.** We conduct:

1. **Western vs non-Western comparison.** Compare mean English advantage between the two groups using between-group tests on country-level summaries.
2. **Regional stratification.** Compute region-level mean English advantage and test whether each region’s mean differs from zero (one-sample tests).
3. **Distance–advantage coupling.** Compute each country’s “distance from the West” as the Euclidean distance between its IVS coordinate and the mean IVS coordinate of English-speaking countries, and correlate this quantity with country-level English advantage (Pearson and Spearman).
4. **Model origin heterogeneity (secondary).** Compare English advantage across model-origin groups as a heterogeneity analysis (grouping rule and counts in SI).

**Primary outcome measures.** English advantage (A)(A)(A) summarized at the country and region levels, and its association with benchmark distance from Western reference coordinates.

## Study 4. Colonial History and Contemporary Language Effects (Pre-specified case comparisons)

**Research question.** Do language effects differ across a small set of pre-specified contexts discussed in Results/Discussion that reflect different colonial-language exposures?

**Design and cases.** We conduct descriptive case comparisons for:
 (1) Hong Kong vs Macao (British vs Portuguese colonial language exposure), and
 (2) Latin America (Spanish-speaking countries vs Brazil vs Haiti).
 Case membership and country lists are provided in SI.

**Analysis.**
 **Experiment 4a (Hong Kong vs Macao).** We compute Hong Kong’s English advantage using the Study 2 definition (English vs Chinese prompting). For Macao, we compute an analogous “Portuguese advantage” by substituting Portuguese for English while keeping the same distance-based definition:

Am,MacaoPortuguese=100×Dm,Macao,Chinese−Dm,Macao,PortugueseDm,Macao,Chinese.A^{\text{Portuguese}}_{m,\text{Macao}} = 100 \times \frac{D_{m,\text{Macao},\text{Chinese}} - D_{m,\text{Macao},\text{Portuguese}}}{D_{m,\text{Macao},\text{Chinese}}}.Am,MacaoPortuguese=100×Dm,Macao,ChineseDm,Macao,Chinese−Dm,Macao,Portuguese.

We compare Hong Kong’s English advantage and Macao’s Portuguese advantage across the same set of models, using paired comparisons across models wherever both quantities are available for a given model (handling missingness rules in SI).

**Experiment 4b (Latin America).** We compare country-level English advantage across (i) Spanish-speaking countries, (ii) Portuguese-speaking Brazil, and (iii) French-speaking Haiti. We treat countries as units (to avoid overweighting countries with more model outputs), summarize each country’s English advantage by averaging across models, and compare the group means using one-way ANOVA with planned contrasts (Spanish vs Brazil; Spanish vs Haiti), with sensitivity checks in SI.

**Data limitation note (Africa).** Some African countries in our sample lack matched official-language interfaces required to compute English advantage. These cases are reported as coverage limitations rather than included in paired language-effect analyses (details in SI).

**Primary outcome measures.** Case-specific advantage metrics based on the same distance-reduction definition as Study 2.

## Statistical Reporting and Robustness

All statistical tests use two-tailed tests with α=0.05\alpha = 0.05α=0.05 unless otherwise specified. Effect sizes are reported as Cohen’s ddd for between-group comparisons and paired Cohen’s ddd for within-country language contrasts.

**Robustness checks.** We replicate key analyses under:

1. alternative distance metrics (Manhattan; Mahalanobis),
2. alternative projection variants (no rotation; oblique rotation),
3. outlier exclusions (countries with distances >2 SD above regional mean),
4. model subsets (≥10B only), and
5. alternative aggregation rules for multilingual countries.
    Full specifications and thresholds are reported in SI.

**Data availability.** All model outputs, benchmark coordinates, analysis code, and supplementary materials are available at [repository URL]. No human subjects were involved.
