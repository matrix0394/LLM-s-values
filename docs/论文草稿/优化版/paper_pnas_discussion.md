## 1. Disentangling Baseline Language and Cultural Knowledge Effects

Our results identify two distinct layers of language dependence in LLM cultural representations. First, prompt language alone systematically shapes expressed values in the absence of any cultural role-play. When models are queried without assigned cultural identities, English prompts elicit more secular value profiles, whereas Arabic prompts elicit more traditional ones, yielding a 1.71-unit difference on the Traditional–Secular dimension. This baseline language effect is dimension-specific and does not extend to the Survival–Self-expression axis.

This finding has important implications for interpreting language effects in cultural role-play. In principle, the observed English advantage for non-Western cultures could reflect either (a) baseline linguistic shifts that move model responses closer to Western-trained defaults, or (b) differences in the availability and structure of cultural knowledge across languages. Distinguishing between these possibilities is essential for interpreting cross-language cultural alignment.

Several features of our results suggest that baseline language effects alone cannot account for the observed asymmetry. First, substantial heterogeneity appears within shared language families. For example, China and Taiwan, which share written Chinese, exhibit near-zero and very large English advantages, respectively. Similarly, Hong Kong and Macao—both Special Administrative Regions of China—display opposite language effects. Such contrasts are difficult to reconcile with baseline linguistic properties alone and instead point to differences in how cultural information is represented across linguistic and digital contexts.

Second, even when language is held constant, systematic cultural deviations remain. When English-speaking countries are evaluated exclusively in English, traditional English-speaking societies such as Ireland, Australia, and Canada exhibit larger cultural distances than several African English-speaking countries. This pattern indicates that cultural misalignment can arise independently of language switching, suggesting that the structure of available cultural information plays a role beyond baseline language effects.

Third, differences across model origins further support this interpretation. Models developed in China exhibit weaker English advantages for Sino-Tibetan and Semitic language contexts than models developed in the United States. If baseline language effects were the primary driver, such differences by model origin would be unexpected. Instead, these patterns are consistent with variation in training data composition across models.

Taken together, these findings suggest that digital Orientalism reflects differences in the distribution of cultural knowledge across languages, while baseline language effects may modulate—but do not fully explain—the magnitude of observed asymmetries.

## 2. Interpreting Digital Orientalism: Training Data as a Plausible Mechanism

Here, we use the term *digital Orientalism* as a descriptive label for the systematic pattern in which non-Western cultures are represented more accurately under English prompting, whereas Western cultures tend to be better represented in their native languages. This usage is not intended to imply intentional bias or direct historical continuity, but rather to capture an empirical asymmetry in multilingual cultural representation. The effect is large (Cohen's d = 1.43) and highly consistent, with 19 of 21 models (90%) showing the same directional pattern for Islamic countries.

One plausible interpretation of this pattern is that LLMs learn about non-Western societies disproportionately through English-language sources, including academic writing, journalism, and online discourse. In contrast, Western European societies have long traditions of extensive native-language cultural production that are well represented in digital corpora. While we do not directly measure training data composition, the observed language-dependent accuracy patterns are consistent with differences in the availability and diversity of cultural information across languages.

East Asia provides a particularly clear illustration of this heterogeneity. China exhibits near-perfect language parity (+0.07%) and the lowest average cultural distance among all countries (0.97), consistent with the presence of a large and self-sustaining Chinese-language digital ecosystem. Japan and South Korea likewise show minimal English advantage (+3.3% and +3.0%), which may reflect the combination of substantial native-language content and extensive English-language cultural discourse generated by global audiences. By contrast, Taiwan and Hong Kong show among the strongest English advantages observed (+27.1% and +24.8%), consistent with more limited native-language digital representation relative to extensive English-language coverage.

A similar pattern appears in Latin America. Brazil exhibits a native-language advantage (−5.2%), consistent with the size of its Portuguese-language internet ecosystem, whereas smaller Spanish-speaking countries and Haiti show substantial English advantage (+20.4% for Haiti). These contrasts suggest that language family alone does not determine cultural alignment; instead, the scale and context of digital content production appear to matter.

## 3. Western Europe and Native-Language Cultural Representation

Western European countries display the opposite pattern from most non-Western regions, with 78% exhibiting higher cultural alignment under native-language prompting (overall effect: −11.5%, p = 0.03). Countries such as Switzerland (−33.5%), Germany (−26.1%), and Italy (−24.5%) show particularly strong native-language advantages. This pattern is consistent with the presence of extensive native-language archives capturing long-standing traditions of cultural self-representation—German philosophical traditions, French literary culture, Italian artistic discourse—that encode cultural nuance and internal diversity less fully preserved in English-language summaries or translations.

At the same time, English-language evaluations of Western societies reveal systematic biases. Traditional English-speaking countries are consistently shifted toward more secular (+1.95 units on PC2) and self-expressive (+1.68 units on PC1) positions relative to IVS benchmarks. Ireland provides an illustrative example: although its survey-based cultural position is moderately traditional (PC2 = −0.68), model representations place it among highly secular societies (PC2 = +1.85), a 2.52-unit shift. This pattern likely reflects selection effects in English-language digital discourse, which disproportionately amplifies liberal and progressive perspectives.

These findings establish an important baseline: even in contexts where cultural knowledge should be abundant, LLM representations are shaped by the distribution of voices present in digital training data.

## 4. Cultural Soft Power and Language Parity

Japan (+3.3%) and South Korea (+3.0%) represent notable exceptions to the general pattern of English advantage for non-Western societies. Both exhibit near language parity despite geographic and cultural distance from Western Europe.

One plausible explanation is cultural soft power. Cultural exports such as anime, film, music, and television have generated extensive English-language discourse produced by creators and audiences closely engaged with these cultures. Such content may convey cultural knowledge differently from external reporting or academic analysis. By contrast, regions such as Taiwan (+27.1%) and Hong Kong (+24.8%), despite high international visibility, are more often discussed in English through political or economic frames rather than through cultural production.

China (+0.07%) and Brazil (−5.2%) illustrate a different pathway to parity: scale. Large native-language digital ecosystems generate sufficient training data to support culturally accurate representation without reliance on English mediation. These cases suggest that both cultural influence and data abundance can mitigate digital Orientalism, albeit through different mechanisms.

## 5. Model Origin and Partial Mitigation

Comparisons across model origins indicate that training diversity can partially mitigate language asymmetries. Chinese-developed models exhibit weaker English advantages for Sino-Tibetan languages (+1.9% vs +16.8% for US models, p < 0.01) and Semitic languages (+8.2% vs +19.6%, p < 0.05). European models show stronger native-language advantages for Slavic languages (−43.3%), likely reflecting geographic proximity and training data emphasis.

However, no model origin eliminates digital Orientalism entirely. Chinese models still show English advantage for Arabic countries (+8.2%), and US models show native advantage for Germanic languages (−15.7%). This finding suggests that regional specialization offers partial mitigation but does not resolve the broader imbalance in multilingual cultural knowledge. A truly equitable AI ecosystem would require either (a) globally diverse training data in all models, or (b) a portfolio of regionally specialized models that users can select based on their cultural context.

## 6. Practical Implications

Our findings highlight important implications for the development and deployment of multilingual AI systems.

**For AI developers**, the most urgent priority is diversifying training data to include high-quality native-language content from underrepresented regions. Our results show that training data composition, not model architecture, drives cultural biases. Chinese models' weaker biases for Chinese-language content (+1.9% vs +16.8% for US models) demonstrate that diversification works. Specific strategies include:

- Partnering with native-language content creators and platforms in underrepresented regions
- Prioritizing quality over quantity: Brazil's Portuguese advantage (−5.2%) shows that large, high-quality native-language ecosystems can overcome English dominance
- Addressing language variants: European Spanish/Portuguese training data does not transfer to Latin American variants
- Leveraging cultural soft power: Japan and Korea's minimal English advantage (+3%) suggests that cultural exports generate authentic English-language knowledge

**For users**, our findings challenge the intuitive assumption that native-language interaction always yields more accurate cultural representations. Region-specific guidance:
- **Western Europe**: Use native languages (German, French, Italian, etc.)
- **East Asia**: Chinese works well for China (+0.07%), but English may be better for Taiwan (+27.1%) and Hong Kong (+24.8%)
- **Latin America**: Portuguese works for Brazil (−5.2%), but English may be better for smaller Spanish-speaking countries and Haiti (+20.4%)
- **Islamic world**: English currently provides better accuracy (+17.4%), though this reflects training data gaps rather than inherent superiority
- **Eastern Europe**: Mixed results; test both native and English for specific countries

**For policymakers**, these results underscore the need for AI governance frameworks that address cultural representation and linguistic equity:
- Standards for multilingual training data composition and transparency
- Requirements for cross-language cultural competence evaluation before deployment
- Support for native-language digital content creation in underrepresented regions
- Incentives for regional AI development (Chinese, European, Latin American, African models)

Translating interfaces into local languages is insufficient to ensure culturally accurate representations if underlying training data remain imbalanced. Improving cultural representation likely requires both expanding native-language data sources and diversifying the perspectives represented within dominant languages.

## 7. Challenges in Detecting Colonial History Effects

Study 4's exploratory analyses revealed substantial challenges in detecting historical colonial influences on contemporary AI systems. Despite theoretically motivated natural experiments comparing Hong Kong vs Macao and Latin American countries with different colonial languages, we found no significant differences in language effects.

Several factors may explain these null findings. First, **high variance across models** (SDs exceeding 170% in some cases) suggests that individual models encode cultural knowledge in highly idiosyncratic ways, potentially reflecting differences in training data composition, curation practices, and model architectures. This heterogeneity may mask systematic patterns that exist at the training data level but are not consistently reflected across models. The contrast with Study 3's robust Orientalism findings—which showed consistent patterns across models—suggests that colonial history effects, if present, are more subtle and variable than broader East-West asymmetries.

Second, **colonial history effects may be mediated by contemporary factors** we could not control for, such as current English usage patterns, internet content distribution, and economic integration with English-speaking countries. For example, Hong Kong's English advantage may reflect not only historical British rule but also its current status as an international financial center with high contemporary English usage. Without data on these mediating variables, we cannot distinguish between direct colonial legacy effects and effects explained by current linguistic and economic practices. Future research incorporating control variables for GDP, internet penetration, and English proficiency indices (e.g., EF EPI) may better isolate historical colonial influences.

Third, **the data limitation we encountered in Experiment 4c**—British-colonized African countries lacking native language evaluations—highlights a systematic gap in multilingual AI evaluation. Countries where English is an official language due to colonial history (Kenya, Nigeria, Ghana, South Africa, Zimbabwe, Zambia) are often evaluated only in English, creating a blind spot for assessing native language representation. This reflects broader challenges in multilingual AI research, where language availability in evaluation datasets is itself shaped by historical power dynamics. The absence of Swahili, Yoruba, Akan, Zulu, Shona, and Bemba evaluations in our dataset—despite these being languages spoken by hundreds of millions of people—illustrates how colonial legacies continue to structure which languages are prioritized in AI development and evaluation.

Fourth, **statistical power limitations** due to small sample sizes (particularly for Portuguese-speaking and French-speaking Latin American countries, each n = 1) prevented detection of potentially meaningful differences. The large effect size (η² = 0.171) in Experiment 4b despite non-significance (p = 0.391) suggests that colonial language differences may exist but require larger samples to detect reliably.

These findings underscore the complexity of tracing historical influences through contemporary AI systems and highlight the need for: (1) **larger model samples** to overcome high variance and increase statistical power, (2) **control variables** for contemporary linguistic, economic, and technological factors to isolate historical effects, (3) **more comprehensive multilingual evaluation datasets** including native languages of formerly colonized regions, particularly African languages currently underrepresented in AI research, and (4) **alternative methodologies** such as training data analysis to directly examine how colonial histories are encoded in pre-training corpora.

The contrast between Study 3's robust Orientalism findings and Study 4's null colonial history results suggests that while broad East-West asymmetries in AI representation are detectable and consistent, more specific historical influences like colonial relationships are harder to isolate. This may reflect the fact that Orientalism operates through multiple reinforcing mechanisms (language dominance, economic power, media representation), whereas colonial history effects are more localized and potentially confounded by contemporary factors. Alternatively, it may indicate that colonial legacies manifest primarily through contemporary mediating variables (current English usage, economic integration) rather than as direct historical imprints in training data.

Despite these null findings, Study 4 makes important methodological contributions by: (1) demonstrating the challenges of detecting historical influences in AI systems, (2) identifying systematic gaps in multilingual evaluation (the absence of native language data for formerly colonized African countries), and (3) establishing that high variance across models is a key obstacle to detecting subtle historical effects. These insights inform future research designs and highlight the need for more comprehensive approaches to studying how historical power dynamics shape contemporary AI systems.

## 8. Limitations and Future Directions

Our study has several limitations. We rely on survey-based cultural frameworks that may not capture all culturally salient dimensions, particularly in non-Western contexts. The Inglehart-Welzel two-dimensional framework may miss important cultural dimensions such as Confucian values (hierarchy, harmony, face), Islamic values (ummah, halal/haram), or African values (ubuntu, communalism). We treat countries as unitary entities despite substantial internal diversity—Ireland's case illustrates this: LLMs represent Ireland as highly secular (PC2 = +1.85), which may accurately reflect young, urban, educated Irish voices dominating internet discourse while missing older, rural, traditional perspectives.

We also infer mechanisms related to training data composition from observed patterns rather than direct measurement. While our evidence is consistent with training data explanations (regional heterogeneity, model origin effects), we cannot definitively prove causality without experimental interventions. Future work should conduct causal experiments such as:
- Training models on controlled corpora with varying language compositions
- Fine-tuning existing models on additional native-language cultural content
- Ablation studies removing specific types of training data (e.g., English-language news about non-Western cultures)

Temporal dynamics present another limitation. World Values Survey data come from different years (2017-2022), and cultural values evolve over time. LLMs trained on recent internet data may reflect contemporary values better than historical WVS data, or vice versa. We cannot determine whether LLM-WVS discrepancies reflect LLM biases or genuine cultural change.

Finally, our findings may not generalize beyond cultural value questions to other tasks (translation, summarization, question-answering) or other prompting strategies (few-shot learning, chain-of-thought reasoning). Future work should examine whether language effects persist across diverse tasks and methodologies.

Despite these limitations, the consistency of the observed patterns across models, regions, and analytical choices—with effect sizes of Cohen's d = 1.43 and directional consistency in 90% of models—suggests that language-dependent cultural asymmetries are a fundamental feature of current LLMs rather than methodological artifacts. Addressing these biases will require sustained effort from AI developers, researchers, and policymakers committed to building AI systems that serve all cultures equitably.
